FLORA Museum Challenge, entry, 8 September 2026

Five Lives
of a Bronze

A Chola bronze was never meant to live in a glass case. She was cast to be dressed in silk, garlanded, and carried through the streets by lamplight. This entry takes the Met's tenth-century Standing Parvati and builds the world around her: five true moments from the thousand years between the mould and the museum.

SourceStanding Parvati, The Met 57.51.3
PeriodChola, Tamil Nadu, ca. first quarter 10th century
Built inFLORA canvas, every iteration
ByHardik Dewra
The piece, final

One bronze, five lives, one strip.

Three passes on the FLORA canvas, every take kept. Pass 1 below is where it started; pass 2 and pass 3 follow the five lives.

Pass 1
Why her

The case removes the life. So I put it back.

Chola bronzes are utsava murtis, processional images. They lived in a sanctum, came out on festival nights dressed in silk and jasmine, and were carried on palanquins through the streets of the Kaveri delta. In a museum she stands stripped, silent and spotlit, and the label says nothing about any of that.

Instead of remixing her surface, the brief here is the second one in the challenge: build the world around it. The room she was made in, the street she was carried through, the pit she was hidden in, the field she was found in, and the gallery where she stands now.

I spend every morning on a practice whose whole point is to see the form of the Goddess. A Chola sthapati saw that form a thousand years ago and poured it into copper. This is that seeing, un-frozen.

The five lives
I

The Mould

ca. 920 CE, a workshop in the Kaveri delta

Lost-wax casting. The wax goddess is coated in clay, the wax melts out, molten alloy pours in, and the mould is broken exactly once. Every Chola bronze is one of one.

Rendered as 35mm film, a documentary from a century that had no cameras.

II

The Procession

ca. 950 CE, a festival night

Silk, jasmine, gold, a thousand oil lamps, drummers, raised palms. This is the life the museum case removes. The hero frame of the series.

Rendered in cinematic colour.

III

The Burial

ca. 1310 CE, before the raids

When the Sultanate armies reached the Tamil country, temple priests buried their bronzes to save them. Many Chola bronzes in museums today were found this way.

Rendered as an oil painting, because this moment survives only as memory.

IV

The Plough

1932, a flooded paddy field

Farmers ploughing kept striking metal. That is how the buried bronzes came back into the world through the twentieth century, one field at a time.

Rendered in black and white, the documentary medium of that decade.

V

Gallery 240

2026, The Met, after closing

The case, the spotlight, and in the glass, faintly, the procession. Her old life is still in the room if you look.

Rendered as museum photography, the medium of now.

Pass 2

Same five lives, stronger model, two takes each.

Pass 1 above was Nano Banana 2 at 1K. Pass 2 re-runs every life on Nano Banana Pro at 2K with tighter art direction: lens, film stock, light and the exact iconography of the bronze (karanda-mukuta crown, kataka mudra, lola-hasta). Two takes per life, both kept. Picks: mould A, procession A, burial B, plough A, gallery B.

Pass 3, with references

One real photograph per life, wired in beside the bronze.

Pass 3 keeps Nano Banana Pro at 2K but adds a second input to every generation: a Pinterest reference for the medium of that life. A Swamimalai sthapati at work for the mould. A South Indian temple lit for a festival night for the procession. Caravaggio's Entombment of Christ for the burial. A Cartier-Bresson frame of rural India for the plough. A Chola bronze in a museum vitrine for the gallery. The prompt tells the model to take the medium, light and composition from the reference and the goddess from the Met image.

Final picks: mould, procession, burial and gallery from pass 3; the plough from pass 2 take A, where she is still lying in the mud being prised out, which is the truer moment.

Craft and process

Everything on the canvas starts from the Met image.

The Met photograph is the source node. Each life is an image-to-image generation wired to it (Nano Banana 2 on FLORA), holding her pose, crown and gesture while the world changes around her. Every scene is rendered in the medium of its own era, so the series reads like an archive that was never kept.

Gallery 240 took two passes. The first lost the reflection idea and produced a plain vitrine. The second put her old life back on the glass. Both stay on the canvas.
